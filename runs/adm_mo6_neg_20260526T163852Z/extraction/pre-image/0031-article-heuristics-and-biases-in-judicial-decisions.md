## Introduction
This article briefly reviews evidence suggesting that in some cases judges could be prone to cognitive fallacies and biases that might affect their judicial decisions. Cognitive biases relating to elements of the hearing process, ruling, or sentencing are reviewed.

## Heuristics and Biases
Heuristics are cognitive shortcuts, or rules of thumb, by which people generate judgments and make decisions without fully considering all relevant information. Various biases such as the availability heuristic and confirmation bias can lead to systemic and predictable biases.

### Cognitive Biases Affecting Judges
1. **Confirmation Bias**: Judges might favor evidence that confirms their preconceived notions and dismiss contradictory information.
   - Example: In a study regarding capital punishment, participants favored studies that aligned with their prior beliefs about the issue.
2. **Hindsight Bias**: People assess outcomes as being more predictable after they have occurred. In legal contexts, this can skew judgments regarding foreseeability.
   - Example: A physician found guilty of malpractice after failing to diagnose a tumor was unfairly assessed using retrospective knowledge that the tumor existed.
3. **Conjunction Fallacy**: More detailed descriptions can lead to higher perceived probabilities of events, even when less detailed information may tell a different story.

## Ruling Process Biases
### Inadmissible Evidence
Studies show that both jurors and judges struggle to disregard inadmissible evidence, leading to biased verdicts even with instructions to ignore such materials.  
   - Evidence impacting judgments includes prior criminal records regardless of relevance.

### Sequential Ruling Bias
Judges making sequential rulings tend to favor the status quo, with their decisions becoming increasingly conservative as they rule through the cases.
  - Research indicates judges’ decisions can be altered by breaks—rulings are more favorable after breaks compared to decisions made at the end of a session.

## Sentencing Process Biases
### Modeling Sentencing Decisions
Investigations show judges often do not consider all relevant factors due to cognitive constraints influenced by the seriousness of offenses.
### Anchoring-and-Adjustment
Judges are susceptible to anchoring effects where the initial numerical estimates can influence sentencing decisions. This occurs even with irrelevant anchors, like those suggested by journalists.

## Conclusion
Heuristic thinking tends to dominate in judicial decision-making, although it may lead to biases in judgments during various phases of the legal process. Awareness of these cognitive influences is crucial for mitigating bias in judicial decisions.
