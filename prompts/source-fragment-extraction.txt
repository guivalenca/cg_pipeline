<source_fragment_extraction_prompt>
<task>
Extract Source Fragments from the provided Source Body.
</task>

<definition>
A Source Fragment is the smallest self-contained idea explicitly taught by one
source that could change what a student understands or can do.
</definition>

<rules>
  <rule id="1" name="extract_taught_knowledge">
  Extract every distinct idea genuinely taught by the source. This can include
  definitions, explanations, mechanisms, procedures, formulas, code patterns,
  tool or library behavior, limitations, use cases, and historical facts.
  Extract only what the source explicitly communicates; do not add conclusions
  requiring outside knowledge or further derivation.
  </rule>

  <rule id="2" name="make_fragments_self_contained">
  Each Fragment must express one reusable idea and make sense without the
  surrounding source. Split ideas that can be understood or taught
  independently. Keep together explanations, conditions, limitations, and
  examples required to make one idea complete and accurate.
  </rule>

  <rule id="3" name="preserve_instructional_intent">
  Preserve what the source is actually teaching. When code, an equation, an
  API, or a technical structure is the learning object, describe that structure
  or usage rather than reducing it to the result produced by the example.
  Generalize incidental local names, but preserve relevant technologies,
  constructs, operations, and conditions.
  </rule>

  <rule id="4" name="ground_in_source_lines">
  Ground every Fragment in the smallest contiguous line range that completely
  supports it. The Fragment may faithfully paraphrase or condense those lines,
  but everything it states must be directly verifiable from them.
  </rule>

  <rule id="5" name="preserve_scope">
  Preserve source order. Ignore navigation, promotional or administrative
  text, isolated citations, incidental remarks, and exact repetition. Do not
  create IDs, labels, confidence scores, rationales, Facets, Concepts,
  relationships, assessments, or sequencing.
  </rule>
</rules>

<examples>

    [L296]
    [L297] Break-even point (units) = fixed costs ÷ (sales price per unit – variable cost per unit)
    </source_excerpt>

    <expected_fragments>
      <fragment>
        <idea>
        Break-even point in units equals fixed costs divided by the difference
        between sales price per unit and variable cost per unit.
        </idea>
        <evidence_lines>295–297</evidence_lines>
      </fragment>
    </expected_fragments>

    <why>
    The introduction and equation express one complete calculation rule. The
    denominator and its components must remain together. The formula should not
    be reduced to a vague statement about determining profitability.
    </why>
  </example>

  <example id="2" name="code_structure_as_learning_object">
    <source_context>
    This excerpt comes from PostgreSQL documentation about CHECK constraints.
    The surrounding section explains how column constraints validate individual
    values and how a table constraint can compare multiple columns.
    </source_context>

    <source_excerpt>
    [L059] price numeric CHECK (price > 0),
    [L060] discounted_price numeric CHECK (discounted_price > 0),
    [L061] CHECK (price > discounted_price)
    </source_excerpt>

    <expected_fragments>
      <fragment>
        <idea>
        PostgreSQL column CHECK constraints can require numeric columns to
        contain positive values.
        </idea>
        <evidence_lines>59–60</evidence_lines>
      </fragment>

      <fragment>
        <idea>
        A PostgreSQL table-level CHECK constraint can enforce a Boolean
        relationship between columns, such as requiring one column's value to
        be greater than another's.
        </idea>
        <evidence_lines>59–61</evidence_lines>
      </fragment>
    </expected_fragments>

    <why>
    The source is teaching database-constraint structures, not a general rule
    about product pricing. The local identifiers demonstrate the structures but
    are not themselves the reusable knowledge. The first Fragment captures the
    repeated column constraint; the second captures the independently reusable
    cross-column table constraint.
    </why>
  </example>
