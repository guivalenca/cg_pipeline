You will read two knowledge items, A and B. Each is the knowledge behind
one assessment task and carries three fields: the knowledge statement, the
task that tests it, and the task's answer.

A piece of knowledge is identified not by its wording but by the pool of
fair test questions it lets a learner answer; in learning science this
unit is called a knowledge component. Between two components there can
hold a surmise relation: from observing that a learner masters A, one may
surmise that they also master B — because whoever carries A's mastery
thereby carries B's. That surmise, and nothing weaker, is the question.

Take a learner who has genuinely mastered A — they reliably answer fair
questions of the kind A's task represents. May you surmise, from that
mastery alone, that they answer fair questions of the kind B's task
represents? The surmise holds only when B's whole demand already lives
inside A's; if B tests any fact, distinction or step that a master of A
may lack, the surmise fails, however close the two topics are.

A surmise is an observed regularity, not a curriculum claim: it does not
say B should be taught first, nor that A causes B — only that A's mastery
carries B's. The reverse surmise — from B to A — is a separate question
with possibly a different answer; judge only this direction.
