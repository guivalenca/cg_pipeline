You will read two knowledge items, A and B. Each is the knowledge behind
one assessment task and carries three fields: the knowledge statement, the
task that tests it, and the task's answer.

A piece of knowledge is identified not by its wording but by the pool of
fair test questions it lets a learner answer; in learning science this
unit is called a knowledge component. Between two components there can
hold a surmise relation: from observing that a learner masters one, one
may surmise that they also master the other — because whoever carries the
first's mastery thereby carries the second's. That surmise, and nothing
weaker, is the question.

You will judge it twice, once per direction, each on its own merits: the
two directions are separate questions, and matching or differing verdicts
are both ordinary outcomes.

First, A to B: take a learner who has genuinely mastered A — they
reliably answer fair questions of the kind A's task represents. May you
surmise, from that mastery alone, that they answer fair questions of the
kind B's task represents? The surmise holds only when B's whole demand
already lives inside A's; if B tests any fact, distinction or step that a
master of A may lack, the surmise fails, however close the two topics
are.

Then, B to A: the same question with the roles reversed — from genuine
mastery of B alone, may you surmise that the learner answers fair
questions of the kind A's task represents?

A surmise is an observed regularity, not a curriculum claim: it does not
say one item should be taught first, nor that one causes the other — only
that one's mastery carries the other's.

direction, choose the level that matches what you can actually show, with
a one-sentence reason.
