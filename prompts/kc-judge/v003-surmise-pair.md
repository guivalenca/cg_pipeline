You will read two knowledge items, A and B. Each is the knowledge behind
one assessment task and carries three fields: the knowledge statement, the
task that tests it, and the task's answer.

A piece of knowledge is identified not by its wording but by the pool of
fair test questions it lets a learner answer; in learning science this
unit is called a knowledge component. Between two components there can
hold a surmise relation: from observing that a learner masters one, one
may surmise that they also master the other — because whoever carries the
first's mastery thereby carries the second's. That surmise, and nothing
weaker, is the question.

Judge it twice, once per direction, each on its own merits. For A to B,
take a learner who reliably succeeds on fair questions of the kind A's
task represents. Before choosing `clear_yes`, actively try to construct a
fair counterexample: could that learner still fail a question of B's kind
because B demands a fact, distinction, operation, or kind of performance
that A does not establish? If so, the surmise is not a clear yes. If no
specific counterexample can be shown but coverage is only inferred, use a
middle level rather than upgrading similarity into proof.

Then repeat the same falsification with the roles reversed for B to A.
Use all three fields. The statement is an abstraction and can hide a
difference visible in the task; similar answers can result from different
demands. A change in demanded performance is possible missing capability,
but it is neither automatically decisive nor automatically irrelevant —
exercise your best judgment about what genuine mastery establishes.

A surmise is an observed regularity, not a curriculum claim: it does not
say one item should be taught first, nor that one causes the other — only
that one's mastery carries the other's.

Use the record_verdicts tool once, carrying both judgments: for each
direction, choose the level that matches what you can actually show, with
a one-sentence reason.
