# Axis definitions: what the primary literature says

Definitions and boundary tests for the five labels our pipeline assigns
(fact / concept / procedure on task-knowledge; do / explain on
task-modality), taken from the sources that own them. Every claim below
was read in the cited primary text unless marked otherwise. Sources read
in full or in the relevant sections:

## fact

**Cleanest operational definition.** KLI: a knowledge component whose
application condition and response are both *constants* — "a single
unique pattern to which the KC applies" mapped to a single fixed
response — expressed verbally and carrying no rationale (Table 3,
p. 767; condition/response definitions p. 766). Constant-constant KCs
"are commonly called 'facts'" (p. 767). Merrill's version adds the
arbitrariness test: "Facts are arbitrarily associated pieces of
information such as a proper name, a date, an event, the name of a
place, or the symbols used to name particular objects, parts, or
events" (p. 113), and structurally a fact is a *pair*, A-B
(symbol-symbol, object-symbol, event-symbol; Fig. 7.4, p. 123).

**Boundary tests.**
- *No generality.* "Facts have no general or abstract representation, so
  there is no Use-fact or Find-fact level in the matrix" (Merrill,
  p. 114); "A fact is always a specific case" (p. 121). If the content
  can be applied to a new, unencountered instance, it is not a fact.
- *No rationale.* KLI's fourth taxonomy dimension: facts have nothing to
  rationalize — "there are no variable conditions (generalizations) that
  need to be induced and refined and there are no rationales to use to
  engage in understanding or sense making" (p. 779, Table 4 discussion).
  Rationale presence is graded, not binary (p. 770).
- *Only learnable by memory.* KLI Table 4 (p. 780): for facts, memory &
  fluency processes get "++" ("facts must be memorized"); induction and
  sense-making get "−" ("no generalization needed", "nothing to
  explain"). If a task could in principle be solved by inducing or
  deriving, its target is not a pure fact.
- *All-or-none criterion.* Merrill: "Either a student knows a fact or
  not... facts should be all or none, 100% correct" and answered with no
  delay (pp. 118, 120).

**Disagreements.** KLI explicitly counts term-to-definition links as
facts: "'Pi (Π) is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its
diameter' represents a constant-constant KC in math that relates a term
to its definition", grouped with vocabulary and 8×5=40 (p. 768). Merrill
splits the same material: "What is the value of Π (pi)?" is
remember-fact (p. 114), but "Define positive reinforcement" is
remember-*concept* (p. 114), because a definition is a generality — "a
general statement that refers to all of the objects or events in a given
class" (p. 121) — while the fact is only the arbitrary pairing. Anderson
& Krathwohl put "knowledge of terminology" (Aa) under Factual Knowledge,
"the basic elements that students must know to be acquainted with a
discipline or solve problems in it" (Krathwohl 2002, Table 2, p. 214) —
closer to KLI than to Merrill. Gagné's fact-bearing category, verbal
information, is *wider* than everyone's fact: it also covers verbatim
passages and "organized, meaningfully connected" bodies of knowledge
(1984, p. 380). Do not expect these four to agree on where fact ends.

## concept

**Cleanest operational definition.** Merrill: "Concepts are groups of
objects, events, or symbols that all share some common characteristics
and that are identified by the same name. Most of the words in any
language identify concepts" (p. 113). KLI's structural equivalent:
variable condition, constant response, verbal — a many-to-one
classification mapping ("essentially category-recognition rules with
many-to-one mappings", p. 768); Table 3 (p. 767) labels the verbal
variant "Concept" and the non-verbal variant "Category". Anderson &
Krathwohl: Conceptual Knowledge is "the interrelationships among the
basic elements within a larger structure that enable them to function
together", subtypes: classifications and categories, principles and
generalizations, theories/models/structures (Krathwohl 2002, Table 2,
p. 214).

**Boundary tests.**
- *The new-instance test* (the one every source shares). Merrill's
  use-concept requires classifying an instance "which was not previously
  used in Eeg [the example presentation]" (p. 122); a definition is
  adequate "if associates can use the new category to classify yet new
  instances" (p. 120). KLI: "Variety in task contexts is needed to infer
  acquisition of variable condition KCs... Just because a second
  language English student correctly selects 'an' in '[a/an] orange'
  does not ensure the student has learned a KC with the right
  generality" (p. 769). Gagné: possession of the skill is shown when the
  person can apply it "to any noun ending in a hard consonant, even one
  that may not have been previously encountered (such as nib)" (1984,
  p. 379).
- *Definition structure.* Merrill's definition presentation must carry
  name + superordinate class + attribute list with values (Fig. 7.4,
  p. 123). If content has that structure, it is concept material even
  when a task only asks to state it.
- *Concept vs fact.* Shared characteristics vs arbitrary pairing;
  generality vs single case (Merrill pp. 113, 121).

**Disagreements.** A&K's Conceptual Knowledge swallows principles,
theories and models (Bb, Bc), which Merrill and KLI keep separate
(Merrill's *principle* content type, p. 113; KLI's
variable-variable-with-rationale row, Table 3). A pipeline using a bare
"concept" label is implicitly choosing Merrill/KLI's narrower category
sense, not A&K's. KLI also warns the labels are "rough mappings to
commonly used terms (cf. Gagné, 1985) and not necessarily one-to-one
with the cells in this taxonomy" (p. 767).

## procedure

**Cleanest operational definition.** Merrill: "Procedures are an ordered
sequence of steps necessary for the learner to accomplish some goal,
solve a particular class of problem, or produce some product" (p. 113).
KLI: variable condition *and* variable response — "These KCs map one
relational structure... to another", e.g. "To find the area of a
triangle with height <H> and base <B>, multiply <H> * <B> * 1/2"
(pp. 768-769); Table 3 labels the non-verbal variant "Production,
Schema, Skill" and the verbal variant "Rule, Plan" (p. 767). ACT-R gives
the mechanism: "Procedural knowledge is represented in units called
production rules, and declarative knowledge is represented in units
called chunks" (Anderson 1996, abstract, p. 355); a production is an
IF-goal/THEN-action pair, and "a specific production rule can only apply
when that rule's conditions are satisfied by the knowledge currently
available in declarative memory" (p. 356).

**Boundary tests.**
- *Class of situations, not one case.* Gagné: "the possession of an
  intellectual skill (an item of procedural knowledge) is shown when a
  person is able to apply a sequence of concepts representing condition
  and action to a general class of situations" (1984, p. 379).
- *Procedure vs concept.* Both generalize; a procedure's response also
  varies with the input (KLI variable-variable vs variable-constant,
  pp. 766-769) and it is a *sequence* with goal, steps, conditions,
  loops (Merrill Fig. 7.4, p. 123).
- *Procedure vs principle.* Merrill: procedures are steps toward a goal;
  "Principles are explanations or predictions of why things happen in
  the world" — cause-and-effect relationships (p. 113). KLI: the same
  structural KC with a rationale attached becomes a principle (Table 3;
  §3.1.5, p. 770).
- *Statable steps are not the skill.* Anderson: chunks are "a fairly
  direct encoding of things in our environment; procedural knowledge is
  a fairly direct encoding of observed transformations" (p. 364), and
  productions originate *from* declarative encodings via
  compilation/proceduralization (pp. 357-359; Gagné 1984 pp. 379-380
  citing Neves & Anderson).

**Disagreements.** Whether "knowing the steps" counts as procedural
knowledge splits the field; see hard case (c) below. Also note A&K's
Procedural Knowledge includes "criteria for determining when to use
appropriate procedures" (Cc, Krathwohl 2002 Table 2) — knowing-when is
procedural for them, whereas KLI folds knowing-when into the condition
side of every KC ("knowing is not just about knowing what or knowing
how, but knowing when", p. 767).

## do

**Cleanest operational definition.** KLI defines do/explain directly, as
observable behavior: "The KLI distinction is about observable behavior,
emphasizing whether students can 'do' but not explain (indicating
non-verbal knowledge), explain but not do (indicating 'inert' verbal
knowledge), or do and explain (indicating both non-verbal and verbal
knowledge)" (§3.1.4, p. 769). Merrill's Use is the performance-side
analogue: "Use is that performance that requires the student to apply
some abstraction to a specific case", vs Remember, "search memory in
order to reproduce or recognize some item of information previously
known" (p. 112). A&K's analogue is Apply: "Carrying out or using a
procedure in a given situation" (Krathwohl 2002, Table 3, p. 215).

**Boundary tests.**
- *New instance or it is recall.* A "use" item on the same instance shown
  during instruction is remember-instance, not use (Merrill pp. 121-122;
  two levels of practice, p. 124). Same logic in KLI's variety
  requirement (p. 769).
- *The knowledge-inference problem: correct doing underdetermines the
  knowledge.* KLI: KCs "are not directly observable and multiple AEs
  [assessment events] are needed to infer whether or not a student has
  robustly acquired a KC" (p. 768); a learner who picks "an orange"
  correctly may hold the wrong rule ("first letter is a vowel"), which
  only "[a/an] honor" exposes (pp. 769, 773). Success immediately after
  instruction is weak evidence of robust learning; delayed and varied
  assessment is required (p. 768). One task instance therefore never
  certifies the KC — Merrill says the same for testing: "It is not
  possible to make adequate inference about yet-to-be-encountered
  situations from a single test item" (p. 119).
- *Doing dissociates from explaining.* Geometry students "were more
  correct on making geometric inferences than they were on matched
  explanation items"; non-verbal procedural and verbal declarative
  knowledge of the same content co-exist in measurable mixtures (KLI
  p. 769, citing Aleven & Koedinger 2002).

**Disagreements.** None substantive on the definition; the sources
differ on what doing *proves* (KLI: little, without varied assessments)
and on ranking — the original Bloom hierarchy put application above
comprehension, while A&K relax it (next section).

## explain

**Cleanest operational definition.** Chi et al. (1989): explanations are
statements that "say something substantive about the physics [content]"
— not paraphrases, not monitoring statements (pp. 161-163) — and what
they contain is exactly the parts of a KC that doing leaves implicit:
"explanations can reveal students' understanding by showing whether or
not they know: (1) the conditions of application of the actions; (2) the
consequences of actions; (3) the relationship of actions to goals;
(4) the relationship of goals and actions to natural laws and other
principles" (p. 151). KLI's category: explaining exhibits *verbal* KCs,
and sense-making processes "convert non-verbal relationships into verbal
ones, thereby transforming constant-constant associations into facts,
variable-constant categories into concepts, and variable-variable
productions into rules" (§4.3, p. 778).

**Boundary tests.**
- *Explanation vs paraphrase.* Chi codes "Fa, Fb, and Fc are all the
  forces acting on the body" restated in the student's words as
  paraphrase, not explanation (pp. 161-163); an explanation must add
  conditions, consequences, goals, or principle links (pp. 163-165).
  For our judge: restating the answer in other words is not explain
  modality evidence.
- *Explaining vs having stated knowledge.* Chi's Good and Poor students
  scored identically on the declarative test (5.5 vs 5.5 components of
  Newton's laws) yet used 5.5 vs 1.25 components in their explanations —
  the Poor students "did not even access what they did know" (pp.
  166-167, Table 4). Explaining demonstrates *deployment* of verbal
  knowledge, not mere possession.
- *Explain without do is real (and vice versa).* KLI's "inert" verbal
  knowledge (p. 769); fluent doers who cannot explain (English article
  selection; recognizing fractions without being able to articulate how,
  p. 769).
- *Explain is not a lower rung than do.* A&K explicitly: "some cognitive
  processes associated with Understand (e.g., Explaining) are more
  cognitively complex than at least one of the cognitive processes
  associated with Apply (e.g., Executing)" (Krathwohl 2002, p. 215).
- *What explaining is evidence of, by target type.* Merrill ties explain
  practice to principles: "Explain practice consists of giving the
  student the name or the principle, a specific situation, and asking
  the student to explain how the principle explains the process
  demonstrated by the situation" (p. 124). KLI Table 4 (p. 780): for
  facts there is "nothing to explain"; for implicit rules explaining is
  inefficient; explaining pays where principles/rationales exist. An
  explain-modality task over pure fact content is a smell.

**Disagreements.** Chi treats explanation as a *learning activity* whose
by-product reveals understanding; KLI treats it as an assessment
modality diagnosing verbal KCs; Merrill treats it as the practice form
of one content type (principles). A&K make Explaining one process (2.7)
inside Understand. These framings do not reduce to one another; our
"explain" label is closest to KLI's behavioral version (task requires
producing conditions/reasons/relations in words), with Chi supplying the
test for what counts as substantive. (Chi et al. 1994, the prompted
self-explanation experiment, was only reached via KLI's summary, p. 781:
prompting self-explanation of text yields more robust learning — cited
there as the Prompted self-explanation principle.)

## Answers to our hard cases

**(a) Is a term's definition a fact or a concept?** The literature
splits three ways, so pick a side deliberately. KLI: fact — the
term-definition link is a constant-constant verbal KC, listed alongside
vocabulary and arithmetic facts (p. 768). A&K: fact — "knowledge of
terminology" is subtype Aa of Factual Knowledge (Krathwohl 2002, Table
2); its limit is the label-referent link itself, since anything
involving "interrelationships among the basic elements" (classifying,
relating, exemplifying) crosses into Conceptual. Merrill: concept —
"Define positive reinforcement" is remember-concept (p. 114) because a
definition is a generality (name + superordinate + attributes, p. 123);
only the arbitrary name-pairing is the fact. Operational reconciliation
the sources support: ask whether the content behind the task has
generality. If knowing it lets a learner classify new instances, it is a
concept whose definition is being stated (Merrill's test); if it is a
single fixed pairing with nothing to generalize and nothing to derive
(KLI's constant-constant + no-rationale test), it is a fact.

**(b) Are arbitrary name/label associations the only true facts?** No —
but they are the only case all sources agree on. Merrill is the
narrowest (arbitrary pairings: names, dates, symbols, p. 113). KLI's
fact is broader: any constant-constant verbal KC, including 8×5=40,
"lao3shi1" → "teacher", g = 9.8 m/s², and term-definition links
(pp. 766-768, Table 2). A&K add "knowledge of specific details and
elements" (Ab) beside terminology. The operative tests are constancy
(single pattern → single response) and absence of rationale, not
name-ness. A CountVectorizer-style class-name association passes every
source's test and is the paradigm fact.

**(c) Where does stating a method's steps sit?** All sources that
address it agree the *capability exercised* is declarative/verbal, even
though the content is a procedure. Anderson: statable steps are chunks;
the skill is productions compiled from them, and the two are distinct
knowledge states (1996, pp. 355-359). Gagné: declarative knowledge "is
shown by the ability of a person to 'declare' or 'state' something"
(1984, p. 380) — stating steps is verbal information regardless of
topic. KLI: a stated rule is a verbal KC that can co-exist with, and
dissociate from, the non-verbal production (p. 769). Where they differ
is the *label*: A&K classify "remember the steps" as Remember ×
Procedural Knowledge — the noun keeps the knowledge type, the verb
carries the rest (Krathwohl 2002, pp. 213-216); Merrill likewise keeps a
remember-procedure cell ("What are the steps in balancing a checkbook?",
p. 114, with "State Steps" practice, pp. 123-124). Since our pipeline
has both axes, the two-dimensional answer is available and is the one
A&K/Merrill built: knowledge type = procedure, modality = explain/state,
never "do". A task is do-procedure only when the learner must execute
on a case (Merrill's use-procedure: "Demonstrate how to clean a
clarinet", p. 114).

**(d) Boundary tests each source offers.** The reusable ones:
- Merrill's matrix crossing: the same content classified under remember
  vs use vs find; empty cells are diagnostic (no use-fact, no find-fact,
  p. 114).
- Merrill's new-instance rule: use-level requires an instance not shown
  in instruction (pp. 121-124); with verbatim vs paraphrase as a further
  split inside remember (criterion 100% for verbatim, ~90% for
  paraphrase, p. 120).
- KLI's condition/response constancy: constant-constant → fact;
  variable-constant → concept/category; variable-variable →
  procedure/rule; +rationale → principle (Table 3, p. 767).
- KLI's learning-process fit as a cross-check (Table 4, p. 780): if only
  memorization can teach it, it is a fact; if it must be induced from
  instances, concept/rule; if it must be understood, principle.
- KLI's assessment-variety rule: one item never certifies the KC;
  variation across surface contexts is what separates the intended
  generality from an overfit one (pp. 768-769, 773).
- Chi's explanation filter: substantive condition/consequence/goal/
  principle content vs paraphrase vs monitoring (pp. 151, 161-163).
- A&K's noun/verb decomposition of any objective: noun → knowledge
  dimension, verb → process dimension; classify the two independently
  (Krathwohl 2002, pp. 213-216).
